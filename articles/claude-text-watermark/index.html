How Claude’s Text Watermark Works

Claude does not hide an identifier inside its answers. Instead, a secret key influences many ordinary token choices until they add up to a detectable statistical pattern.

August 2026 16 min read

In August 2026, Anthropic announced that future Claude models would add a watermark to the text they generate. This watermark is unlike the translucent label we usually see on an image: readers cannot see it, and no hidden characters are added to the text. It is a statistical pattern distributed across many token choices. No single word reveals it; the trace emerges only when many choices from a longer passage are analyzed together.

This article is based primarily on Anthropic’s official explanation, with technical context from the SynthID-Text paper and Sebastian Raschka’s walkthrough.

1. Quick Overview

What is the watermark?

When a large language model generates the next token, it often has to choose among several candidates that are almost equally probable—and equally reasonable. Claude’s watermarking mechanism uses a secret key to control the randomness behind those choices, much like fixing a random seed so that the same random sequence can be reproduced. Across a long response, many small choices accumulate into a detectable statistical pattern.

The watermark is not a mysterious serial number or a hidden character. Removing formatting, copying the text, and pasting it somewhere else does not remove the underlying pattern.

Does watermarking affect quality?

Anthropic says its internal tests found no practical effect on the content, creativity, or readability of Claude’s responses, and users are not expected to distinguish watermarked responses from unwatermarked ones. Claude uses a version of Google DeepMind’s SynthID-Text. DeepMind compared watermarked and unwatermarked responses in live Gemini traffic and found no statistically significant difference in thumbs-up or thumbs-down rates. Human raters likewise found no meaningful quality difference in controlled comparisons.

Those results do not prove that watermarking can never affect any output. They show that the system is designed to alter the selection only when several candidate tokens are similarly good, and that testing did not reveal a decline in content quality.

Does it make Claude slower or more expensive?

The watermark does not generate extra tokens, so it does not add cost by making responses longer. Anthropic says its performance overhead is negligible and that the price of using the model does not change.

Can the watermark identify a user?

No. Claude’s text watermark indicates possible Claude involvement; it contains no user, organization, account, or chat information. Detecting it can at most show that Claude probably helped generate or process the text. The watermark itself carries no identity data.

Will Claude be the only model with a watermark?

No. Anthropic introduced watermarking because the European Union requires transparency measures for AI-generated content. Other major model providers signed the same Code of Practice and will use their own marking systems. Providers may use different algorithms and keys, so Claude’s detector cannot identify text generated by every other company’s models.

2. How the Watermark Is Embedded

To understand Claude’s watermark, we only need to locate the point at which it enters the generation pipeline.

Given the tokens already present, a language model computes a score for each possible next token and selects one of the plausible candidates. We do not need to revisit tokenization, logits, softmax, top-k, or top-p sampling here. The important point is that at many positions the model is not choosing between one correct word and a collection of wrong words. It is choosing among several alternatives that would all work.

Anthropic uses this example:

The weather today was cold and…

The next word is unlikely to be sugary, but both overcast and grey are plausible. Under ordinary sampling, the difference barely matters to the meaning, so randomness can decide which one appears.

Watermarked generation still selects only from words the model already considers reasonable. The difference is that the choice is no longer driven by arbitrary randomness: it is influenced by a secret key together with a few words that came before. Someone who knows the key can later test whether a sequence of token choices is consistent with that rule.

This does not mean Claude always prefers overcast or always prefers grey. The same word may behave differently in a different context because changing the preceding words changes the pseudorandom result derived from the key. Nor does the watermark force a word the model would barely consider, such as Anthropic’s deliberately obscure example nubilous, into the answer.

The watermark therefore does not live inside a special word, and there is no fixed “Claude watermark vocabulary.” What can be detected is the statistical pattern created by many tokens in their particular contexts.

SynthID-Text

Claude’s watermark is a version of SynthID-Text, published by Google DeepMind in 2024. This family of methods goes back to an idea proposed by Scott Aaronson in 2022. They share a central principle: do not retrain the model and do not append information after generation. Instead, change the source of randomness at the point where the model samples its next token.

The core relationship can be simplified as follows:

\[ \text{watermark score} =f(\text{secret key},\text{recent context},\text{candidate token}) \]

The function combines the secret key, recent context, and the candidate token to produce a score that looks random but can be reproduced. The same key and inputs give the same result; change the context or the candidate token and the result changes too.

In simplified form, the model obtains candidate tokens according to its original probability distribution and compares them over multiple rounds. The watermark uses several key-controlled scoring functions, with a different function deciding which candidates survive each round until a final token is selected.

Think of it as a knockout tournament. Every entrant must first be a candidate the language model considers reasonable. The scoring functions influence the tournament result, but they do not turn an implausible token into a good answer. One result looks unremarkable; after many such choices, watermarked text is more likely than ordinary text to match the secret key’s pattern.

Anthropic also explains the idea with Monopoly and the digits of pi. Normally, players roll dice to decide how far to move. Imagine replacing the dice with digits read sequentially from a randomly chosen location in pi. The moves would still look random to the players and the game would feel the same. But anyone who knew pi and the starting location could later check whether the recorded moves matched that sequence.

Watermarked Claude text works in much the same way. Readers still see natural prose, while someone with the key can test whether the token choices across the passage could have come from that particular key-controlled random process.

3. How Can We Tell Whether Text Was Mostly Generated by Claude?

A simple but impractical detector would recover the original prompt, run Claude again, reconstruct the candidate-token scores at every position, and compare them with the text under review. This would be expensive, and in most real cases the detector does not know the original input or even the exact model version.

One important feature of SynthID-Text is that detection does not require rerunning the underlying language model or knowing the original prompt.

The detector tokenizes the text, examines the context before each token, and combines that context with the secret key and the watermark scoring functions. It computes how closely each token at each position matches the watermark rule, then aggregates the scores across the passage.

In unwatermarked text, high and low scores should behave roughly like ordinary random outcomes. In text produced by a watermarked Claude model, outcomes favored during generation appear more often, so the aggregate score may depart significantly from the unwatermarked baseline.

As a simple illustration, imagine a detector with three watermark scoring functions. Each token receives a sequence of zeros and ones. The detector sums the scores at many positions, takes an average, and compares the result with a threshold. The real SynthID-Text detector is more sophisticated, but the core idea is the same: it evaluates a statistical tendency across the passage, not a hidden string embedded in one location.

Detection is therefore not an absolute yes-or-no test. Longer passages usually provide more samples and greater confidence about Claude’s involvement. A very short passage may contain too little information and produce only an uncertain result. If Claude generated a long passage from scratch, the signal has many opportunities to accumulate. If Claude changed only a few grammar or punctuation details in a human draft, most tokens still came from the original author and the signal may be too weak to detect.

The detector also cannot strictly distinguish “Claude wrote this from scratch” from “Claude heavily rewrote a human draft.” If Claude selected enough new tokens, both cases can produce a detectable watermark. Anthropic frames the detector’s question accordingly:

What is the likelihood this was partly written by Claude?

Conversely, failing to detect a Claude watermark does not prove that a human wrote the text. It may have come from another AI model, an older unwatermarked model, or a passage that was edited enough to damage the watermark. Even another SynthID-Text model will generally use a different key, so Claude’s detector cannot verify its watermark.

Anthropic will not publish the secret key directly. If a third party obtained it, that party could not only detect the watermark but potentially forge it or target the positions that contribute the most evidence. Anthropic instead says it will offer a watermark detection API.

Watermark detection also differs from existing AI-text classifiers. Ordinary detectors do not possess model providers’ keys; they infer AI involvement from sentence structure, word preferences, predictability, and other stylistic clues. A watermark detector checks a key-related pattern deliberately introduced during generation. It is more targeted, but also narrower in scope.

4. Limitations

Most limitations of Claude’s text watermark follow from the same fact: it needs many freely chosen tokens to accumulate statistical evidence. The fewer choices the model has—or the more those choices are later changed—the harder detection becomes.

Factual passages and short samples

Anthropic uses this example:

Isaac Newton’s most famous work was called Principia…

The next word, Mathematica, is part of the correct title and cannot be swapped merely to strengthen a watermark. Factual answers, exact numbers, equations, and proper nouns often present the same problem: the correct choice is clear, leaving little room for the watermark to act without sacrificing accuracy.

Short text has a different problem: too few samples. A real watermark tendency may be hidden by ordinary random variation. It is like trying to decide whether a coin is biased: two or three identical flips prove very little, whereas hundreds of flips can provide meaningful evidence.

Proofreading and light editing

If a user asks Claude to correct only grammar and punctuation in a human-written passage, Claude may choose very few new tokens. The watermark can exist only in those changes and may be too weak to detect.

The more Claude rewrites, the more new choices it makes and the more room the watermark has to accumulate. Detection can therefore indicate probable Claude involvement, but it cannot tell us from the watermark alone whether Claude generated the whole passage or deeply edited an existing draft.

The countermeasure: rewrite with another model

Text watermarking has an unavoidable weakness: prose can be rephrased. Changing a few words, deleting a few sentences, or making minor edits may leave much of the signal intact. Heavily changing wording, syntax, and paragraph structure disrupts the original tokens and their contextual relationships, reducing the detector’s confidence.

If an attacker knew which positions contributed most to the watermark score, editing those positions could efficiently weaken the signal. Without the key, however, the attacker does not know which tokens matter most and must edit broadly enough to destroy the pattern by chance.

One likely workaround is to generate an article with a powerful Claude model and then pass it to a smaller model that does not use the same watermark. The second model need not solve the original task; it only needs to change wording and sentence structure. That may substantially weaken Claude’s watermark.

Watermarking cannot technically prevent this workflow, but it adds another model call and another editing stage. Broad rewriting performed solely to remove the watermark can also reduce quality: precise, natural phrasing may be replaced by language that is more awkward or less accurate.

Translation requires the same distinction. A translation generated directly by Claude can carry a watermark because Claude selects every token in the translation. If an already watermarked passage is translated by another system, however, the original token sequence is replaced and the original watermark may be substantially weakened.

Anthropic is explicit about this trade-off: light editing may not remove the watermark completely, while a complete rewrite that replaces every word can. Once every word and sentence structure has changed, whether the result should still be described as the original AI-generated text becomes partly a question of definition.

5. Code, Images, and Other Files

Code

Text watermarking works best where several expressions are equally acceptable. Code often requires exact output. Keywords, function names, variable references, and syntax cannot be changed arbitrarily without breaking the program.

Anthropic uses 2 + 2 = as an example. If the model is completing the calculation, the next token should clearly be 4. If it is discussing George Orwell’s Nineteen Eighty-Four, a particular context might call for 5. The watermark does not replace a correct answer with an incorrect one merely to leave a trace.

Code follows the same rule. Positions with one clearly correct or best choice are not nudged, so code generally carries less watermarking than natural-language prose. Comments, variable names, and equivalent implementations may still offer choices, allowing the watermark to act there with little practical effect on the code itself.

Images and other files

When Claude creates supported files such as .png, .jpg, or .svg, Anthropic uses C2PA content credentials rather than the statistical text watermark described above.

A C2PA credential is a cryptographically signed record attached to a file’s metadata. It says that Claude generated or processed the file, and C2PA-aware tools can read and verify it. Like the text watermark, the credential does not identify a particular user or organization; it records Claude’s involvement with the file.

The two mechanisms are fundamentally different. A text watermark is a statistical pattern in token choices and produces a probabilistic detection result. A C2PA credential is a signed metadata record. Copying and pasting text may preserve its statistical pattern, while screenshots, re-exports, or metadata removal may strip a file credential.

Claude’s text watermark should therefore not be understood as a label attached to a document file, and C2PA metadata in an image should not be treated as the same watermarking algorithm.

6. Why Introduce a Text Watermark?

Anthropic is introducing text watermarking to comply with the European Union’s transparency requirements for AI-generated content. The watermark will apply globally at launch because the company does not yet have a durable way to limit it to the EU. Anthropic also plans to add watermarking over the following months to older models released before August 2, 2026, which are covered by a transition period.

From a regulatory and platform-governance perspective, a watermark provides provenance evidence for AI-generated text. It will not stop someone who deliberately tries to evade detection, and it cannot replace fact-checking, copyright analysis, or human review. It can, however, make direct or lightly edited model outputs easier to identify when they spread at scale.

A text watermark can establish only one thing: a passage may have been generated or processed by Claude at some point. It cannot determine who the author is, whether the content is true, or who bears responsibility for it.

In short, Claude’s watermark is hidden in a series of apparently ordinary choices. No single choice proves anything. Only when many choices collectively match a pattern produced by the secret key do they form a detectable statistical signal.
